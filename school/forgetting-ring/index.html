01 What you are looking at

A ring of 4 coins, each heads or tails. The ring has 2W configurations, and the bars under the beam give each one a weight. Even configurations (an even number of tails) hang from the left pan; odd ones from the right.

At the start the weights are 1 + c on every even configuration and 1 − c on every odd one. With c = 1, all the weight is even and the beam is pinned left. The parity lean q is (even − odd) ÷ (even + odd): +1 is all even, 0 is level.

Each step applies one mixing matrix T to all the weights at once. The beam levels, but not like a pendulum settling. The lean is multiplied by the same number every step. On the log chart that is a straight line, and the dots ride it until the arithmetic itself runs out of digits.

02 How the model is built

The principle was supplied, not found. At the close of the previous exercise the owner ordered that this one stand on mathematics Einstein would hand over. He answered the commission (ticket 20261104-spider-1) with a theorem from his own notebook, checked by the Lean kernel: T_closed, T_spectrum and mass_gap in Z2Strip.lean, recorded in his STATUS file as stage S3. His sentence for visitors:

“Apply this mixing step to a ring of W coins, and any lean toward even or odd parity shrinks by exactly the same factor every step, tanh(β)W, no matter where you start.”

The matrix comes from its definition. The page never types in T. For every pair of configurations σ, σ′ it sums over all 2W settings of a hidden ring τ: T(σ,σ′) = Στ exp(β Σx σxσ′xτxτx+1). At W = 8 that is 16,777,216 terms. The theorem says the sum collapses to (2 cosh β)W + (2 sinh β)W·(parity of σσ′), and the strip under the coins shows the built matrix and that formula agreeing to round-off.

What the theorem says. For β > 0, every eigenvalue of T is r = (4 cosh β)W, l = (4 sinh β)W, or 0. The flat vector 𝟙 has eigenvalue r, the parity vector p has eigenvalue l, and they are at right angles. The start 𝟙 + c·p therefore stays on those two directions, and qt = c·(l/r)t = c·tanh(β)W·t.

What the page measures. The lean, and the shrink factor qt ÷ qt−1, are read from the weights after repeated multiplication by the built T. They are rescaled each step so the largest weight is 1. The theorem's tanh(β)W is printed beside the measurement as a prediction; it never feeds the iteration. At β = 1, W = 4 the measured factor is 0.336429764386 at every step, and the lean first drops below 10⁻³ at step 7, as predicted.

Where the measurement stops. A double-precision number keeps about 16 digits. Below a lean of 10⁻⁸ the measured factor can wander by more than 10⁻⁶, so it is shown as noisy. Below 10⁻¹³ the lean itself is round-off, so the trace is greyed and the run stops. If the dots seem to stop falling there, that is the arithmetic, not the coins.

What it does not say. Einstein's file calls log(r/l) a “mass gap”. Here it is only the ratio of two eigenvalues of a finite matrix on a ring of coins. It says nothing about Yang–Mills, four dimensions, or continuum limits. The exact rate belongs to this one lean: a quantity with no parity part has nothing to forget, and the theorem is silent on everything else. The crossing step ⌈ln(10⁻³/|c|) ÷ (W ln tanh β)⌉ is arithmetic on the theorem, not a separate proof. And the result is classical: this model has been solved exactly for a long time. What is new is a machine-checked proof in Mathlib.

Two edges. At β = 0 the matrix wipes the lean in a single step and the theorem's hypothesis fails, so the slider stops at 0.05. At W = 1 the ring is a single coin whose neighbour is itself, a self-bond. The formula still holds there, and the page says so when you choose it.

03 Where this shows up

A transfer matrix turns a chain of interacting parts into repeated multiplication by one matrix. The ratio of its two largest eigenvalues then sets how fast information at one end is forgotten further along. This is the standard way physicists read a correlation length off a one-dimensional model.

The same shape appears wherever a process is repeated: a shuffle, a noisy channel, a Markov chain. The gap between its first and second eigenvalues fixes the per-step forgetting factor. Here that gap is known in closed form, so you can watch the measurement land on it.
