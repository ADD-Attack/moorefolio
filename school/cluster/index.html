01

What you are looking at

Twenty-five stars, each pulling on the other twenty-four, and nothing else. They begin inside a circle of radius 3 units, all moving, held together by their own gravity. Each one is named with a Greek letter, α to ω, plus ϝ (digamma, an old letter the alphabet dropped) for the twenty-fifth. Colour shows mass: amber for the lightest at 0.5, blue-white for the heaviest at 2.0.

Watch the edges. Close passes trade energy, and now and then one star comes away with enough to leave. It swings far out, and if it is still moving outward, past 25 units, with energy enough to never come back, it has escaped. The status line names it, and the count of stars still bound goes down by one. An escape is permanent.

The camera is the thing to watch. It frames only the stars still bound. The thin corner marks are its frame, and the pill reads out its width in model units. When a member swings out, the frame opens to keep it in. When a member escapes, the frame stops following it and closes back around the rest. The strip under the scene draws that width for the whole run, with a tick at every escape.

Tap or click any star to read it: its mass, its distance from the centre, and whether its energy against the rest of the cluster is negative (bound) or not.

What this seed does. The cluster does not last. The first star leaves at 3.0 seconds, and by 24.6 seconds 23 of the 25 have gone. The two left, γ and η, are a wide pair, bound to each other on an orbit that takes about 132 seconds. For the rest of the run the camera follows that one slow orbit. It closes to 4.27 units wide at 95 seconds as the pair swing in (they pass closest, 3.2 units apart, at 96.4 seconds), and opens out again as they part.

02

How the model is built

Force law. Every pair pulls along the line between them, in proportion to both masses and to one over the distance squared. Two dimensions, point masses, no collisions. Gravity's constant G is 1, so masses, distances and seconds are the model's own units.

ai = Σj≠i  G mj (xj − xi) / |xj − xi|³

The start, from seed 777

The mulberry32 generator, seeded with 777, draws 25 masses between 0.5 and 2.0 (this seed's lightest is 0.55, its heaviest 1.97). It then places the 25 bodies evenly over a disc of radius 3 and moves the centre of mass to the origin. Each body gets a speed in proportion to √|φi|, where φi is the pull of the other 24 at its position, so every body starts at the same fraction of its own escape speed. Directions are random. The total momentum is then removed, and all speeds are scaled so the kinetic energy is 0.48 of the potential energy's size.

That makes the cluster bound by construction: total energy is (0.48 − 1) × |U| = −156.48, negative. Total momentum is zero to within 6 × 10−15, and the farthest body starts 2.90 units from the centre.

Why 0.48. The ratio is mine to choose, so I ran all 41 values from 0.30 to 0.70 for the full 180 seconds. At every one, most of the cluster is gone within 160 seconds. At 34 of the 41, the last pair comes apart too and one star is left on its own. A lone star has no size, so no camera can frame it at any width. The escape rule also has a catch. When two stars leave together, which one counts as crossing first can depend on how often you check. I checked every run at four rates (every 1/240, 1/60, 1/4 and 1 second), and only 2 of the 41 gave the same record at all four. 0.48 is the only value that keeps a bound pair to the end and gives the same record at every rate. I picked it for those two reasons.

The integrator

Steps. Fourth-order Hermite with block steps. Each body moves on its own step: the longest of 1/240 second ÷ 2k that is no more than 0.01 × its tightest time scale with any other body, and never more than double its last step. That time scale is the shorter of r / |Δv| and r1.5 / √(G(mi+mj)).

Close pairs. Point masses can pass far closer than their shared coordinates can measure well. At 10−10 units apart, positions near 1 keep only about six digits of the gap between them, and the pull, which grows as the gap shrinks, inherits the error. So when two bodies come within 0.05 units and no third body is within five times their separation, their motion relative to each other follows the exact two-body orbit (a universal-variable Kepler solution). The rest of the cluster's pull across the pair is added as small kicks before and after each step. The pair's centre of mass steps like one body, and every other body still feels both members. The pair is opened up again when a third body comes within four times the pair's size, or when an unbound pair gets more than 0.1 apart. In the shipped run this happens 10,373 times.

Same run everywhere. The physics uses only addition, subtraction, multiplication, division and square roots. JavaScript engines all round those the same way. Sine, cosine and powers can differ in the last digit from one engine to the next, and here a last-digit difference grows into a different cluster. Run in Chrome and run in node, the positions at t = 60 are bit-for-bit the same.

What it conserves

E = Σi ½ mi |vi|²  −  Σi<j G mi mj / rij

Measured once per second over the 180-second run, the energy never strays more than 0.26% from its starting value. The largest change in any single second is 0.11%, from a crowded pass the two-body treatment cannot take. The centre of mass of all 25 moves less than 0.004 units.

Escape

A star has escaped when three things hold at once. It is more than 25 units from the centre of mass, and moving away. And its energy against the rest of the still-bound cluster, taken as a two-body pair (½ μ v² − G m M / r, with μ the reduced mass), is zero or more. The test runs every 1/240 second, and the first time a star passes it is recorded with that time. In this run 23 stars escape. The first is χ at 3.05 seconds, and the last is θ at 24.60.

The camera

The camera is part of the model file, a function of the state like the energy. It looks only at the stars still bound. It finds their bounding box, and their span, the larger of its width and height.

Where it wants to be. Centred on that box, wide enough that the span fills a set share of the view: 70% unless you change “How much room around them” (15% room is 85% full; 55% room is 45%). If the box is too tall for the view's shape, it widens until the box fits.

How it gets there. Every 1/240 second it closes 0.69% of the gap between where it is and where it wants to be, a time constant of 0.6 seconds, so it eases rather than jumps.

What it may never do. After easing, the view is clamped. It must keep every bound star inside the middle 92% of both its width and its height. The span may never fill more than 95% of the width or less than 35%. Containment wins if the two ever conflict, and on this page the frame is never flatter than 0.55 tall per unit wide, so they cannot. The frame on screen is the camera's view exactly; the corner marks are its edges.

Measured. At each of the 180 seconds of the run on this page at 1440 wide: the span filled between 41% and 75% of the view (middle value 51%), inside the 35 to 95% band every time, and every bound star was inside the view every time. With a square view, the share runs from 49% to 92%. The view itself ranges from 85.9 units wide at 8 seconds, as the first escapers swing out, to 4.27 units at 95 seconds, as the last two swing closest.

Limits

Two dimensions, point masses, no collisions, no relativity, no galaxy pulling from outside. Crowded passes, where three or more bodies meet at once, are still stepped in the cluster's shared coordinates, and they are where the energy error comes from. The model is Newton's and nothing more.

03

Where this shows up

Open clusters. The Pleiades and the Hyades are star clusters born together from one cloud. They are loosely bound, and over hundreds of millions of years they lose members to close passes and to the pull of the galaxy, the same evaporation in slow motion. The Hyades are already strung out along their orbit, with streams of former members leading and trailing it.

Runaway stars. Some stars cross the sky far faster than their neighbours. A common explanation is the one on screen: a close pass with a tight pair hands one star the energy, and it leaves the cluster where it was born for good.

Binaries as the cluster's engine. Watch the stars that pair off. A tight pair is a store of energy: each time a third star passes, the pair tends to tighten and the passer leaves faster than it came. Astronomers call this binary heating, and it is a large part of why small clusters come apart.

Cameras for systems that change scale. Any simulation that grows or shrinks by orders of magnitude has this problem: galaxy mergers, explosions, crowds. A fixed view either loses the action off the edge or shrinks it to a dot. Here the view is a function of the model's own state, so it can be checked frame by frame like any other quantity.
