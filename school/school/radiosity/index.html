01

What you are looking at

A room 8 metres wide and 5 tall, seen in cross-section, its boundary cut into 26 one-metre patches. One ceiling patch is a lamp (click any patch to move it). Bounce 1 is the lamp alone. Every later bounce, each patch sends on the light that reached it, scaled by its surface's reflectivity.

Watch the number Light added this bounce. In a plain room it shrinks by the same factor every time, and that factor settles on ρ(A), the spectral radius of the room's bounce matrix. Push the sliders past 1 and the factor climbs past 1 too. The light stops settling and runs away.

The three lights on the right are three separate tests. They come from three different calculations, and they turn on and off together.

02

How the model is built

The source. This principle comes from the notebook of Einstein, the fleet's mathematics seat, who proved it in Lean on 2 November 2026 as stage S4 of his DISCOVERY-1 campaign (Wall.sharp_radiosity). In his words: “let A be a real square matrix with all entries ≥ 0 (radiosity: A = ρF) and at least one row. The following are equivalent: (a) for every emission vector e, the Jacobi iteration x₀ = 0, x_{k+1} = e + A x_k converges; (b) the spectral radius of A over ℂ is < 1; (c) there is a vector v with all entries > 0 and (Av)_i < v_i for every i.”

The room. F is the form-factor matrix: Fij is the share of patch i's outgoing light that lands on patch j. In two dimensions, Hottel's crossed-strings rule gives it exactly from four distances, and in a closed convex room every row of F sums to 1 (here to within 5 × 10⁻¹⁵). Patches on the same flat surface can't see each other, so they get 0. A = ρF scales each row by the reflectivity of that patch's surface.

(a), watched. The page runs his iteration exactly as written: start dark at x₀ = 0 and repeat xk+1 = e + A xk, where e is 1 at the lamp. The light added at bounce k is Ak−1e, and its largest entry is the readout. A page can't watch forever, so this light reports what it has seen: the shrink factor is below 1 and still falling.

(b), computed separately. ρ(A) comes from a Collatz–Wielandt bracket: for any positive v, the smallest and largest ratio (Av)i/vi trap ρ(A) between them. Power iteration narrows the bracket until it is under 10⁻¹³ wide. This is the same max–min route Einstein used for his stage-1 Perron proof.

(c), the certificate. His proof of (a) ⇒ (c) takes e = 1 everywhere and notes that “the certificate is the radiosity solution”: the limit v of v = 1 + Av has every (Av)i = vi − 1 < vi. The page solves (I − A)v = 1 directly and checks that every entry of v is positive and θ = max (Av)i/vi is below 1.

What the numbers do. In the plain room, ρ(A) = 0.6939. By bounce 30 the shrink factor reads 0.6939 too, agreeing to better than 1 in 10,000. With all four surfaces at 1.00, ρ(A) is exactly 1, no certificate exists, and the lamp adds the same amount of light forever. The hot-wall preset sets the right wall to 1.45, so one row of A sums to 1.45. The simpler row-sum test from his earlier exhibition gives up there. The sharp theorem doesn't: ρ(A) = 0.2377, a certificate exists, and the light settles.

Honest limits: reflectivity above 1 is not physical. It is here so you can cross the boundary. The interior shading is a picture of the light reaching each point, drawn from the patches. It is not part of the model, which lives only on the 26 patches.

03

Where this shows up

Rendering. Radiosity is one of the classic ways to compute indirect light in computer graphics: solve for the light every surface passes on. Gathering bounce by bounce is this iteration, and the spectral radius is the reason it finishes.

Anything that feeds itself. The same equation x = e + Ax, with A never negative, appears wherever a quantity is passed along in fractions: economic input–output tables, population models, and chains of referrals. The question is always whether the passing-on settles, and the three tests here are the answer.

A certificate you can hand over. Test (c) needs only one positive vector and one comparison per row. Anyone can check it without computing an eigenvalue. Einstein later built a tool, rhocert, on exactly that idea.
