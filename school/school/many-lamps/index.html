01

What you are looking at

The room from the first page: 8 metres by 5, its boundary cut into 26 one-metre patches. Now you can add bulbs, spotlights and lasers in any colour, at any brightness, anywhere inside, and the walls have colours too. Each lamp throws its light onto the patches once. That is bounce 1. After that the room takes over, and every bounce each patch passes on what reached it.

Colour runs as three separate rooms that share one shape: a red one, a green one and a blue one. A red wall reflects plenty of red and little else. Light that leaves it lands pinkish on the white walls next to it. That is colour bleeding, and the Colour bleed scene shows it arriving bounce by bounce.

The verdict under the room runs the theorem once per colour. In Channels split the walls reflect more red than they receive, so red runs away while green and blue settle. The theorem is saying three independent things about three independent rooms.

01½

Little tiny walls

The Tile size slider cuts the same boundary finer: 1 m, 50 cm, 25 cm, 12.5 cm, 6.25 cm, which is 26, 52, 104, 208 or 416 patches. Every setting rebuilds the form factors exactly over the new tiles and re-aims every lamp at the new walls. The cost is the honest part: each bounce, every patch reads every other, so the work per colour goes from 676 calculations to 173,056.

The light is the same light at every setting. Only the tiles shrink. Each tile is drawn by its brightness per metre, not its total, so a half-size tile with half the light looks the same as the big one. Per-metre brightness moves less than 3% from the coarsest grid to the finest, and ρ moves less than 0.01%. Squint and the rooms match. What changes is the detail: at 6.25 cm the laser's lit patch is 6.25 cm wide, a real spot instead of a metre of wall.

Adaptive spends the tiles where they matter. It starts from the 26 one-metre patches and halves a tile, down to 6.25 cm, where a laser lands or a spotlight's cone edge falls. It also halves tiles to 25 cm where the settled light jumps by more than ×2 between neighbours. A split is undone only after four lamp moves without being needed, so the mesh doesn't flicker as lamps move. The form factors use the same crossed-strings rule over the uneven tiles, which is exact for unequal pairs. The laser still lights exactly one 6.25 cm patch, with about a third of the patches of 6.25 cm everywhere.

The guarantees don't get cheaper or dearer with the grid. The a-priori budget depends on the largest reflectivity, not on the patch count, so the guaranteed number of bounces is the same at every tile size.

02

How the model is built

The theorem governs the room; lamps live in the emission. Einstein's theorem (DISCOVERY-1 S4, Wall.sharp_radiosity) is about the matrix A: the iteration xk+1 = e + A xk settles for every emission e exactly when ρ(A) < 1, exactly when a certificate exists. Lamps only change e. Moving, recolouring or dimming a lamp never changes whether a colour settles. Only the surfaces do that.

Colour. Each channel c has its own matrix Ac = diag(ρc)F, where a surface's ρc is its reflectivity times its colour in that channel. F is the exact crossed-strings form-factor matrix from the first page. Each channel runs its own iteration, and each gets its own ρ, certificate and verdicts.

Lamps. A lamp's light lands on the patches according to a distribution d, the share falling on each patch, with d ≥ 0 and Σd = 1. A lamp adds brightness × colourc × d to ec. From any point inside, the 26 patches split the full circle of directions between them. A bulb sends equal light in every direction, so each patch gets its angle's share of the full turn, and every patch gets some. A spotlight does the same across a 40° cone (20° each side of its aim), and only patches inside the cone get light. A laser puts all of it on the one patch its beam hits. The ceiling panel is the first page's lamp: a glowing patch whose own light is bounce 1.

What the numbers do. The Original room is the first page's default reproduced bit for bit: ρ = 0.6939 in all three colours, and by bounce 30 the shrink factor reads 0.6939 too. Because every step is linear, two lamps together give exactly the sum of each lamp alone (here to within 2 × 10⁻¹⁶). In Channels split every surface reflects 1.10 of red, 0.88 of green and 0.605 of blue, so ρ is 1.10, 0.88 and 0.605.

How the picture maps brightness. The numbers are exact; the picture is a display of them. Each bounce, the brightest patch in any colour, not counting patches that are themselves lamps, becomes the screen's white point W. A patch with value x in a colour is drawn at log(1 + 8x/W) ÷ log 9 of full brightness in that colour. So no wall patch is ever clipped, and a patch with more light always looks brighter. Only a ceiling panel, which is a lamp, can go past white. The glow inside the room is drawn on the same curve from the light arriving at each point, scaled to its own brightest point and capped at 40% of full brightness so the walls, which carry the numbers, stay the brightest things in the picture. A runaway room never washes out, because W grows with it. Hover or tap a wall to read its true values.

Honest limits: lamps are pure emitters. They don't block or reflect light. Direction matters only on the first flight; after that, light is tracked per patch, not per ray. The cone and beam drawn on the room are pictures of that first flight. Reflectivity above 1 isn't physical; it is here so you can cross the boundary.

03

Things to try

Superposition. Put a red laser and a blue spotlight on the same wall. Where they overlap, the patch reads the red one plus the blue one, exactly. Light adds.

Brightness can't break it. In the Original room, turn the lamp up to 3. Everything gets three times brighter, and the shrink factor and every verdict stay where they were, because those belong to the room.

A colour that runs away. Paint one wall pure red and push its reflectivity past 1. Watch the red verdict turn while green and blue stay settled. Or start from Channels split and pull all four reflectivities down to 0.90: red settles too.

04

What the programme learned today

Two instruments, not one. The certificate bracket reads the settled light: if its top end θ is below 1, settling is certified. It brackets ρ loosely (0.6168 to 0.7300 in the Original room), so it is the wrong tool for measuring ρ. The bounce differences are the right one. By bounce 20 in the Original room their bracket is 3 × 10⁻⁵ wide around 0.6939.

A fence around the answer. When every difference ratio is below 1, the remaining bounces can only add between m/(1−m) and M/(1−M) times the last difference. The settled light is fenced in, and the fence tightens every bounce. If even the smallest ratio reaches 1, the light cannot settle, and the page says divergence certified.

A budget you can promise in advance. From the brightest surface alone, pmaxk+1/(1−pmax) ≤ τ guarantees the answer in any room: 54 bounces for the Original room's 0.85. This room actually needed 14. The gap is the price of knowing nothing about the geometry.

Gauss–Seidel. GS uses each patch's new light as soon as it is computed. It usually needs fewer sweeps: 9 against Jacobi's 14 here. That is a measurement, not a theorem. Stein–Rosenberg guarantees only that GS is no slower. Its stop is certified anyway: the residual r = e + Ax̃ − x̃ costs one extra pass, and ‖r‖/(1 − c) bounds the true error.

A design margin. Capping every reflectivity at 0.90 caps the brightness at 1/(1 − 0.9) = 10× the light the lamps put in directly, and it makes every room settle. In Channels split it brings red down from 1.10 to 0.90, and red settles.

A coarse certificate. Treat each of the four surfaces as a single patch, using its worst patch. If that 4 × 4 room settles, the 26-patch room does too. In the Original room ρ(Q) = 0.9731, far above the true 0.6939 but still below 1. In Colour bleed, red's ρ(Q) is 1.087. The coarse test fails there while the fine room settles: that is the looseness price.

06

Live, and the moving lamps

Two ways to play. From dark is the theorem’s run: the room starts dark and every bounce is counted. Live keeps the light that’s already on the walls. Each frame the lamps move one tick, then the solver runs at most 3 bounces (or 3 Gauss–Seidel sweeps) starting from where the light already is. That is a warm start. The light chases the lamps rather than starting over.

Honest while chasing. The certified error bound ‖e + Ax − x‖/(1 − c) is computed for the scene as it stands at that tick, so it always tells you how far the walls are from the light this exact arrangement would settle into. Turn Lamps move off and the bound falls bounce by bounce until the walls catch up.

The motion is arithmetic, not animation. Every lamp’s position and aim at tick t come from t alone: orbits on ellipses, spotlights turning full circles, a laser swinging ±55°. The same tick always gives the same room. Drag a moving lamp and it stops where you drop it.
